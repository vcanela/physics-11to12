1. Speed and acceleration

Speed is how fast something is moving: the distance it covers each second.

v = Δd ÷ Δt    units: m s−1

The Δ (delta) means “change in”. When speed varies during a journey, this formula gives the average speed.

Velocity is speed in a particular direction. A car going round a corner at a steady speed has a changing velocity, because its direction is changing. At Year 11 the two words are often used loosely; at Level 2 the difference matters.

Acceleration is how quickly the speed changes: the change in speed each second.

a = Δv ÷ Δt    units: m s−2

Slowing down gives a negative acceleration, often called deceleration.

Worked example

A bus speeds up from 5.0 m s−1 to 15 m s−1 in 4.0 s. Find its acceleration.

  1. Δv = 15 − 5.0 = 10 m s−1 final speed minus starting speed
  2. a = Δv ÷ Δt = 10 ÷ 4.0 = 2.5 m s−2
  3. The bus gains 2.5 m s−1 of speed every second.

The classic trap: a flat line means “not moving” on a distance graph, but “constant speed” on a speed graph. Always read the axis labels first.

Explorer: build a motion story

Add up to eight 3-second steps, then press Play to watch the car and both graphs. Before you press Play, try to predict what each graph will look like. This is for exploring, so nothing is marked.

Worked example: from story to graphs

A cyclist starts from rest and speeds up steadily for 10 s, reaching 6.0 m s−1. She rides at that speed for 20 s, then brakes steadily and stops 5 s later.

  1. 0 to 10 s: speeding up. The speed graph slopes up; the distance graph curves, getting steeper.
  2. 10 to 30 s: constant speed. The speed graph is flat; the distance graph is a straight sloping line.
  3. 30 to 35 s: slowing down. The speed graph slopes down to zero; the distance graph levels off until it is flat.
  4. Notice the distance graph never goes down: she never travels backwards.

3. Forces

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). We draw forces as arrows: the direction shows which way the force acts, and the length shows how big it is.

The net force is the total of all the forces on an object. Forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract.

  • Balanced forces (net force = 0): the object stays still, or keeps moving at constant speed in a straight line. This is Newton’s first law. Balanced forces do not mean “not moving”.
  • Unbalanced forces (net force ≠ 0): the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. Newton’s second law tells you how much:

Fnet = ma

Mass and weight

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, in kilograms. It is the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on the object, in newtons:

Fg = mg

On Earth g = 10 m s−2, but on the Moon g = 1.6 m s−2. So an astronaut’s mass stays the same while their weight changes.

Friction and air resistance

Friction acts between surfaces and opposes motion. Air resistance is friction from moving through air, and it gets bigger as speed increases. That one fact explains terminal velocity.

Worked example: net force and acceleration

A 1200 kg car has a driving force of 3000 N and friction of 600 N acting on it. Find its acceleration.

  1. Up and down: weight and support force are equal, so they cancel. the car doesn’t sink or float
  2. Fnet = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N forward
  3. a = Fnet ÷ m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2.0 m s−2 forward

Worked example: the falling parachutist

This story appears in exam after exam, so it is worth knowing step by step.

  1. Just after jumping: speed is low, so air resistance is small. Weight is bigger than air resistance, so the net force is downward and she accelerates.
  2. Falling faster: as speed increases, air resistance increases. The net force gets smaller, so her acceleration gets smaller. She is still speeding up, just less each second.
  3. Terminal velocity: air resistance grows until it equals her weight. The forces are balanced, the net force is zero, and she falls at a constant speed.
  4. Parachute opens: the large area makes air resistance suddenly much bigger than her weight. The net force is upward, so she slows down.
  5. New terminal velocity: as she slows, air resistance decreases until it equals her weight again. She falls at a new, much lower constant speed.

4. Pressure

Pressure tells you how concentrated a force is: how much force pushes on each square metre.

P = F ÷ A    units: pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N m−2

  • The same force on a smaller area gives a bigger pressure. This is why knives are sharp and snowshoes are wide.
  • When something rests on the ground, the force is usually its weight, so you will often need F = mg first.

The area trap: 1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m2 = 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm2. To convert cm2 to m2, divide by 10 000, not by 100.

Worked example

A 60 kg person stands on both feet. Each foot has an area of 150 cm2 touching the floor. What pressure do they exert on the floor?

  1. F = mg = 60 × 10 = 600 N
  2. A = 2 × 150 = 300 cm2 = 300 ÷ 10 000 = 0.030 m2 two feet
  3. P = F ÷ A = 600 ÷ 0.030 = 20 000 Pa = 2.0 × 104 Pa

5. Energy, work and power

Energy is never created or destroyed. It only changes from one form to another.

This is the most important idea in physics. In mechanics, the two forms you calculate are:

  • Gravitational potential energy, stored because of height: ΔEp = mgΔh
  • Kinetic energy, the energy of movement: Ek = ½mv2

When an object falls with no air resistance, potential energy turns into kinetic energy and the total stays the same. In real life, some energy becomes heat and sound because of air resistance and friction.

Work is done when a force moves something. It is the amount of energy transformed:

W = Fd    and    W = ΔE    units: joules (J)

The distance must be in the same direction as the force. Lifting something, the force is its weight and the distance is the height.

Power is how fast work is done:

P = W ÷ t    units: watts (W), where 1 W = 1 J s−1

Worked example

A 0.50 kg ball is dropped from a height of 5.0 m. Ignoring air resistance, how fast is it moving just before it hits the ground?

  1. Ep at the top = mgΔh = 0.50 × 10 × 5.0 = 25 J
  2. All of this becomes kinetic energy, so Ek at the bottom = 25 J conservation of energy
  3. v = √(2Ek ÷ m) = √(2 × 25 ÷ 0.50) = √100 = 10 m s−1
