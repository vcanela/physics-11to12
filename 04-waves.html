1. What a wave is

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter with it. As a wave passes, the particles of the material (the medium) move back and forth around one spot, then settle back where they started.

  • Transverse waves: the particles move at right angles to the direction the wave travels. Examples: ripples on water, a wave on a rope, light.
  • Longitudinal waves: the particles move back and forth along the direction the wave travels, making squashed regions (compressions) and stretched regions (rarefactions). Example: sound.
  • Mechanical waves, such as sound and water waves, need a medium to travel through.
  • Electromagnetic waves, such as light and radio, can travel through empty space.

2. Describing waves

QuantityMeaningUnit
Amplitude, Athe biggest displacement from the rest position; bigger amplitude means more energym
Wavelength, λthe length of one complete wave, for example crest to crestm
Frequency, fthe number of waves passing a point each secondhertz (Hz)
Period, Tthe time for one complete wave to passs
Speed, vhow fast the wave travelsm s−1

v = fλ      T = 1 ÷ f

An important idea for later: the source sets the frequency, and the medium sets the speed. When a wave passes into a different medium, its frequency stays the same, so its speed and wavelength change together.

Worked example

Four complete waves pass along a rope in 2.0 s. Each wave is 1.5 m long. Find the frequency, the period and the speed.

  1. f = 4 waves ÷ 2.0 s = 2.0 Hz
  2. T = 1 ÷ f = 1 ÷ 2.0 = 0.50 s
  3. v = fλ = 2.0 × 1.5 = 3.0 m s−1

3. Sound

  • Sound is a longitudinal wave made by something vibrating. It needs a medium, so it cannot travel through empty space.
  • It travels faster in solids than in liquids, and faster in liquids than in gases. In air it travels at about 340 m s−1.
  • Pitch depends on frequency: higher frequency, higher pitch. Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Sound above that is ultrasound.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude: bigger amplitude, louder sound.
  • An echo is sound reflected from a surface. The sound travels there and back, so the distance to the surface is half the total distance travelled.

Worked example

You shout towards a cliff and hear the echo 0.60 s later. How far away is the cliff?

  1. Total distance travelled by the sound: d = vt = 340 × 0.60 = 204 m
  2. That is there and back, so the cliff is 204 ÷ 2 = 102 m away
  3. To 2 s.f.: 1.0 × 102 m

4. Light and reflection

Light is an electromagnetic wave. It travels at 3.0 × 108 m s−1 in a vacuum, and it travels in straight lines, so we draw it as rays.

Visible light is one small part of the electromagnetic spectrum. In order of increasing frequency (and decreasing wavelength): radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. They all travel at the same speed in a vacuum.

The law of reflection

The normal is an imaginary line drawn at 90° to a surface, where the ray hits it. Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the surface.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection    (i = r)

Images in a flat mirror

When you look in a flat mirror, the reflected rays reach your eye. Your brain traces them back in straight lines, so the image appears to be behind the mirror. The image is:

  • the same size as the object, and upright,
  • the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front,
  • virtual: no light actually comes from where the image appears to be,
  • laterally inverted: left and right are swapped.

Worked example

A ray hits a mirror at 25° to the mirror’s surface. Find the angle of reflection.

  1. The angle is given from the surface, but we need it from the normal.
  2. i = 90° − 25° = 65°
  3. r = i = 65°

5. Refraction

Light travels more slowly in glass and water than in air. When light crosses into a new material at an angle, the change in speed makes it change direction. This is refraction.

  • Going into a material where it is slower (air into glass), light bends towards the normal.
  • Going into a material where it is faster (glass into air), light bends away from the normal.
  • A ray travelling along the normal does not bend, although it still changes speed.
  • The frequency stays the same, so the wavelength changes along with the speed.

Picture a car driving at an angle off a road onto sand. The wheel that reaches the sand first slows down first, so the car turns. Light does the same at a boundary.

Total internal reflection

When light goes from glass or water into air, it bends away from the normal. If the angle of incidence is big enough (bigger than the critical angle), the light cannot get out at all, and all of it reflects back inside. This is total internal reflection. Optical fibres use it to carry light, and internet data, along thin glass threads, even round bends.

Dispersion

Different colours of light slow down by slightly different amounts in glass, so they bend by different amounts. A prism spreads white light into a spectrum, and raindrops do the same to make a rainbow.
