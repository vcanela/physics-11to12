1. Charge and static electricity

All matter contains positive protons and negative electrons. Usually there are equal numbers, so an object is neutral. Charge is measured in coulombs (C). One electron carries a charge of −1.6 × 10−19 C.

  • Charging by friction: rubbing two materials together moves electrons from one to the other. The object that gains electrons becomes negative; the one that loses electrons becomes positive. Only electrons move: protons are locked inside the nucleus.
  • Forces between charges: like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Charged objects attract neutral ones. A charged object pushes or pulls the electrons inside a nearby neutral object, so one side of it becomes slightly charged. This is called induced charge.
  • Conductors, such as metals, let electrons move through them. Insulators, such as plastic and rubber, do not.
  • Earthing: connecting a charged conductor to the ground lets electrons flow to or from the Earth until the object is neutral.

Worked example

A balloon rubbed on hair gains 5.0 × 1012 electrons. What is its charge?

  1. charge = number of electrons × charge of one electron
  2. = 5.0 × 1012 × 1.6 × 10−19 = 8.0 × 10−7 C
  3. It gained electrons, so the charge is negative: −8.0 × 10−7 C

2. Current, voltage and resistance

Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point. It is measured in amperes (A), where 1 A is one coulomb per second.

I = qt

By convention, current flows from the positive terminal of the battery, around the circuit, to the negative terminal. The electrons actually move the other way, but we use the conventional direction.

Voltage (also called potential difference) is the energy carried by each coulomb of charge. The battery gives energy to the charge; components such as lamps use it up. It is measured in volts (V), where 1 V is one joule per coulomb.

V = Eq

Resistance is how strongly a component opposes the current. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

V = IR

Circuit symbols

Measuring current and voltage

  • An ammeter measures current. It goes in series, so the current flows through it.
  • A voltmeter measures voltage. It goes in parallel, connected across the component, so it compares the energy of the charge before and after.

Ohm’s law, and why lamps break it

For a resistor at constant temperature, the current is proportional to the voltage: double the voltage and the current doubles. A graph of voltage against current is a straight line through the origin, and its gradient is the resistance. This is Ohm’s law.

A lamp filament is different. As more current flows, the filament gets hotter and its resistance increases. Its graph curves instead of staying straight.

Worked example

A lamp connected to a 12 V supply has a current of 0.50 A through it. (a) Find its resistance. (b) How much charge flows through it in one minute?

  1. (a) R = VI = 120.50 = 24 Ω
  2. (b) q = It = 0.50 × 60 = 30 C one minute = 60 s

Adding a resistor in series makes the total resistance bigger. Adding one in parallel makes it smaller, because you have given the current an extra path. That second idea surprises most people, so it is worth thinking about.

Worked example: series

Find the current, and the voltage across each resistor.

  1. RT = 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0 Ω
  2. I = VRT = 126.0 = 2.0 A the same through both resistors
  3. V1 = IR1 = 2.0 × 4.0 = 8.0 V
  4. V2 = IR2 = 2.0 × 2.0 = 4.0 V
  5. Check: 8.0 + 4.0 = 12 V ✓ the voltages share the supply

Worked example: parallel

Find the current in each branch, the total current, and the total resistance.

  1. Each branch has the full 12 V across it.
  2. I1 = 124.0 = 3.0 A    I2 = 126.0 = 2.0 A
  3. IT = 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 A
  4. RT = VIT = 125.0 = 2.4 Ω
  5. Check with the formula: 1RT = 14.0 + 16.0 = 0.4167, so RT = 2.4 Ω ✓ less than 4.0 Ω

4. Power and energy

Power is how fast energy is transformed, measured in watts (W). For electrical components:

P = IV    and    P = Et

Combining P = IV with V = IR gives two shortcuts that you will use a lot at Level 2: P = I2R and P = V2R.

Kilowatt hours

A joule is tiny, so power companies charge by the kilowatt hour (kWh): the energy used by a 1 kW appliance running for one hour.

energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h)     1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J

New Zealand mains electricity is 230 V.

Worked example

A 2300 W heater runs on 230 V mains for 3.0 hours. Electricity costs 30 cents per kWh. (a) Find the current. (b) Find the cost.

  1. (a) I = PV = 2300230 = 10 A
  2. (b) Energy = 2.3 kW × 3.0 h = 6.9 kWh 2300 W = 2.3 kW
  3. Cost = 6.9 × 30 = 207 cents = $2.07

5. Magnetism and electromagnetism

Magnets and fields

  • Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.
  • A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where magnetic forces act. We draw it with field lines.
  • Field lines go from north to south outside the magnet. They never cross, and where they are closer together the field is stronger.
  • A compass is a small magnet. Its north end points along the field lines.

Electricity makes magnetism

A current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it, in circles centred on the wire. To find its direction, use the right-hand grip rule: point your right thumb in the direction of the current, and your fingers curl in the direction of the field.

On diagrams, ⊙ (a dot) means current coming out of the page, like the tip of an arrow coming towards you. ⊗ (a cross) means current going into the page, like the tail feathers of an arrow going away.

Electromagnets

Winding the wire into a coil, called a solenoid, adds the fields from every turn together. The field looks just like a bar magnet’s. To find which end is north, curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the current around the coil: your thumb points to the north end.

An electromagnet is stronger with:

  • more current,
  • more turns of wire,
  • an iron core inside the coil.

Unlike a permanent magnet, an electromagnet can be switched off. That is what makes it useful in scrapyard cranes, door locks, speakers and MRI scanners.
