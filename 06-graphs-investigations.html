6. Graphs and Investigations

How to plan an experiment, handle data, draw graphs by hand, and turn a curve into a straight line. This is the toolkit for the Level 2 practical investigation.

About the answers. Calculation questions have a “Check your number” button. The number is the least important part: your tutor will look at your working, and on this page, at your graphs. When you read a gradient from a hand-drawn line, your answer will differ slightly from anyone else’s. That is expected, so the checks give a sensible range.

You will need graph paper. If you print this page, the last sheet is a page of graph paper.

1. Planning a fair test

  • The independent variable is the one you choose and change.
  • The dependent variable is the one you measure, to see how it responds.
  • Controlled variables are everything else that could affect the result. Keep them the same, and say how you did it.

Range and number of values. Use a wide range of the independent variable and at least six different values, spread evenly. A curve is impossible to spot from three points.

Repeats. Measure each value at least three times and take the average. Repeats reduce the effect of random errors and let you spot a reading that doesn’t fit.

Reducing uncertainty. When a measurement is small compared with your reaction time or the scale divisions, measure many at once. For example, time ten vibrations, then divide by ten. Your reaction-time error is spread across all ten.

Worked example

A student investigates how the length of a guitar string affects the pitch of the note it plays.

  1. Independent: the vibrating length of the string (she changes it by pressing at different frets).
  2. Dependent: the frequency of the note, measured with a phone app.
  3. Controlled: the same string, the same tension (no retuning), plucked at the same point.
  4. Values: six lengths from 0.20 m to 0.65 m, with three readings at each length.

2. Recording data

  • Independent variable in the first column, then the repeats, then the average.
  • Put the quantity and its unit in the heading, such as “length (m)”. Do not write the unit in every cell.
  • Record every value to the same number of decimal places, matching the precision of your instrument.
  • If one repeat is far from the others, it is probably an outlier. Measure it again if you can; if not, leave it out of the average and say so.

3. Graphs and straight lines

Drawing a graph by hand

  • Independent variable on the horizontal (x) axis, dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and unit, just like the table headings.
  • Choose scales that use most of the page and go up in easy steps (1, 2, 5 or 10 per square, never 3 or 7).
  • Plot each point as a small neat cross.
  • Draw a line of best fit: one smooth straight line or curve that passes as close as possible to all the points, with about as many above as below. Never join the dots.

Reading a straight line

A straight-line graph has the equation

y = mx + c

where m is the gradient and c is where the line crosses the y axis (the intercept). If the line goes through the origin, c = 0 and y is proportional to x: double x and y doubles.

Find the gradient from your line, not from two data points, and use a large triangle covering most of the line. Always give the gradient its units, and write the final equation with the real physics symbols, not y and x.

Worked example

A beaker of water is heated steadily and its temperature recorded each minute.

time (min)012345
temperature (°C)202631384349
  1. The points lie close to a straight line that does not pass through the origin.
  2. Large triangle on the line, from (0 min, 20 °C) to (5 min, 49 °C):
  3. gradient = (49 − 20) ÷ (5 − 0) = 5.8 °C per minute the rate of heating
  4. intercept = 20 °C the starting temperature
  5. Equation: T = 5.8t + 20, with T in °C and t in minutes

4. Recognising curves

Many relationships in physics are not straight lines. At Level 2 you need to recognise four common shapes. In each, k is a constant.

The curves for 1/x and 1/x2 look similar, as do x2 and a steep straight line over a short range. You usually cannot tell them apart by eye, which is why the next section matters.

A quick test: think about what happens when x doubles.

RelationshipWhen x doubles, y …
y ∝ xdoubles (× 2)
y ∝ x2quadruples (× 4)
y ∝ √xincreases by × 1.41 (√2)
y ∝ 1/xhalves (× ½)
y ∝ 1/x2becomes a quarter (× ¼)

5. Straightening curves

A curve is hard to read accurately, and hard to tell apart from other curves. A straight line is easy. So physicists linearise: they choose what to plot so that the graph becomes a straight line.

If you suspect y = kx2, plot y against x2 instead of against x. If you are right, you get a straight line through the origin, and its gradient is k.

If you suspect …plot y against …and the gradient is
y = kx2x2k
y = k√x√xk
y = k/x1/xk
y = k/x21/x2k

Worked example

A toy car is launched along carpet at different speeds, and its stopping distance is measured.

speed v (m s−1)0.51.01.52.02.53.0
distance d (m)0.060.190.460.781.271.79
v2 (m2 s−2)0.251.002.254.006.259.00
  1. The first graph curves upward. Doubling the speed from 1.0 to 2.0 m s−1 roughly quadruples the distance (0.19 to 0.78 m), which suggests d ∝ v2.
  2. Plot d against v2. The points lie on a straight line through the origin, which confirms it.
  3. gradient = (1.80 − 0) ÷ (9.00 − 0) = 0.20 s2 m−1 from the line, using a large triangle
  4. Equation: d = 0.20v2

6. Writing it up

A Level 2 investigation report usually follows this order. Your teacher will give you the exact requirements, but the structure is much the same everywhere.

  1. Aim: what you are investigating, in one sentence.
  2. Variables: independent, dependent and controlled, and how you controlled each one.
  3. Method: clear enough for someone else to repeat, including how you reduced uncertainty.
  4. Results: a well-set-out table with repeats and averages.
  5. Graphs: the raw graph, then the straightened graph with its line of best fit.
  6. Equation: the gradient with units, and the final equation in physics symbols.
  7. Discussion: what the relationship means physically, sources of error and uncertainty, any outliers, and how you would improve the experiment.

The discussion is where the higher grades are decided. Use the skills from Writing explanations: link each source of error to how it affected your results.
